A Short History of Financial Euphoria: Framework + Case Studies

Completed

A Short History of Financial Euphoria is a short book written by Galbraith in 1990, prompted by the 1987 stock market crash. It connects six financial speculation cases over 350 years, from the 1637 Tulip Mania to the 1929 Great Crash. He aimed to prove that behind these seemingly unrelated crashes lies a recurring mechanism: short financial memory, wealth mistaken for wisdom, "innovation" always being a repackaging of leverage, and only individuals being punished after a crash, without examining speculation itself. The conclusion is pessimistic—he believes no system can cure this mechanism, and the only defense is for individuals to remain vigilant against excessive optimism.

The three stages of speculative euphoria—psychological drive, structural amplification, and outcome obfuscation—form a self-reinforcing loop, which is why history repeats itself. Market dogma itself refuses to acknowledge this, leading to the fact that it is never institutions that can truly defend against it, but only an individual's sensitivity to "excessive optimism."

Framework

The essence of speculative euphoria is the repeated invention of leverage—no matter how the outer shell changes, the underlying mechanism is always the repackaging of "creating debt collateralized by real assets (to varying degrees)."

Essence: The Repeated Invention of Leverage

"Financial operations themselves do not possess the ability for true innovation"—so-called innovation is essentially a variation of the same thing: using real assets to support increasingly large debts.

Two modes of leverage:

  • Single-layer leverage: Mortgaging real assets to borrow money to buy speculative items, with bounded risk.
  • Circular/nested leverage: Refinancing and nesting layers using the book value appreciation of the speculative items themselves, exponentially amplifying risk.

Reason: Why Leverage Can Be "Reinvented" Repeatedly Without Being Detected

  • Short financial memory: Public memory usually does not exceed 20 years. Old disasters are forgotten, and a new generation emerges with the arrogance of "this time is different."
  • → Leading to the illusion of novelty: Precisely because the last time is forgotten, another repackaging of leverage can be seen as an "unprecedented discovery."

Euphoria: Two Mechanisms Sustaining Euphoric Expansion

  • False association of money = wisdom: Wealth is mistaken for proof of intelligence; those who make money believe their judgment is superior and are excessively praised. This self-assurance leads people deeper into euphoria.
  • Self-protection and rejection of dissent: In euphoria, skeptics are seen as incompetent, unimaginative, or ill-motivated. The entire financial community forms a collective force to protect the euphoria, suppressing sober voices.

These two points together explain why, once euphoria starts, it is difficult to stop it from within—participants have both the motivation of self-assurance and mechanisms to suppress external doubts.

Outcome: Post-Crash Pattern

  • Scapegoating: Anger is directed at specific individuals (e.g., John Law, Milken) or specific tools (e.g., program trading), but never at the collective irrational optimism itself.
  • External narrative: Blaming the collapse on external factors (e.g., production slowdown in 1929, budget deficit in 1987) to maintain the belief that "the market is inherently perfect."

This step is the closing point of the entire chain: accountability remains focused on specific people and tools, leaving the starting point of the chain intact. The next round of leverage reinvention merely changes the asset target and begins anew.

Case Studies

Tulip Mania (Netherlands, 1636-1637)

Tulip bulb trading was originally a niche market for enthusiasts, but rare varieties gradually became status symbols. In the 1630s, prices soared, with a single rare bulb sometimes costing as much as a house. Many buyers mortgaged properties and land to borrow money to buy bulbs, hoping to make a quick profit. In early 1637, prices began to fall without warning; no one was willing to buy, panic spread rapidly, and the market collapsed within days, leading to a severe economic depression in the Netherlands.

  • Factual Elements:
    • Asset: Rare varieties of tulip bulbs.
    • Central Figures: The author notes the absence of specific "individual genius" records in this case, but the collective was dominated by a "desire for wealth."
    • Form of Leverage: Single-layer leverage. Mortgaging real assets like houses and land to borrow money to buy bulbs.
    • Trigger Point: Unknown. Panic spread when "wise or timid people" began to sell off.
    • Post-crash Accountability: Angry buyers collectively defaulted, and courts ruled the contracts gambling and unenforceable.
  • Underlying Logic:
    • "New Thing" Illusion: Tulips, as exquisite and rare species, captured the public's imagination for this "beautiful investment."
    • Suppression of Dissent: The entire Netherlands fell into a social pressure where "no astute person wished to be left behind."
    • External Scapegoating: Extremely distressed victims sought scapegoats everywhere, avoiding discussion of the fundamental cause of mass frenzy.

This was the first recorded large-scale mass delusion in history, proving that even the most improbable things can become a medium for euphoria.

John Law and the Royal Bank (France, 1716-1720)

John Law was granted permission to establish the Royal Bank to issue banknotes to repay national debt. Initially, banknotes could be exchanged for coins at any time, and the system worked well, leading to increasing issuance. To support the value of the notes, he founded the Mississippi Company, claiming to have discovered gold mines in French Louisiana (without actual evidence). The company's stock offering was met with frenzied demand. The entire cycle was: issue banknotes → use banknotes to buy Mississippi stock → strong stock demand → issue new shares → stock price rises again → issue more banknotes. In 1720, Prince Conti demanded to exchange a large sum of banknotes for gold, exposing the bank's insufficient coin reserves, triggering a chain of runs. Banknotes and stock collapsed simultaneously, and John Law fled in haste.

  • Factual Elements:
    • Asset: Mississippi Company stock and banknotes issued by the Royal Bank.
    • Central Figures: John Law (hailed as a genius combining honesty and talent before the crash).
    • Form of Leverage: Circular/nested leverage. The bank over-issued banknotes on meager coin reserves; banknotes bought stock, and rising stock prices supported further banknote issuance.
    • Trigger Point: Prince Conti, unable to acquire more stock, retaliated by demanding to exchange a large amount of banknotes for gold.
    • Post-crash Accountability: John Law was fiercely condemned, forced into exile, and died in poverty.
  • Underlying Logic:
    • "New Thing" Illusion: The concept of a land bank and the "innovation" of paper money replacing coins.
    • Suppression of Dissent: The surge in wealth "bought off the intelligence of participants"; no one wanted to question whether there was really gold in Louisiana.
    • External Scapegoating: For a century afterward, the French remained skeptical of "banks" themselves, rather than reflecting on the speculative instincts of the time.

South Sea Bubble (Britain, 1711-1720)

The South Sea Company was founded in 1711 to take over the British government's massive debt accumulated during the War of the Spanish Succession. The government paid the company 6% interest, and the company gained the right to issue stock and a charter nominally covering trade with South America—Spain simultaneously claimed sovereignty over the region, and the company was actually only allowed "one ship a year" trade, making it almost unprofitable. What truly drove up the stock price was the imaginative potential brought by the charter: the stock price rose from £128 in January 1720 to around £1000 by summer. Confidence then began to waver, coupled with insiders cashing out early, and the stock price plummeted to £124 by year-end, about one-seventh of its peak.

  • Factual Elements:
    • Asset: South Sea Company stock.
    • Central Figures: Robert Harley (Earl of Oxford) and John Blunt.
    • Form of Leverage: Exchanging government public debt for company stock, using the 6% interest paid by the government as the base for leverage (backed by national credit).
    • Trigger Point: Profit-taking by internal senior management and informed parties.
    • Post-crash Accountability: Extremely harsh. Several MPs were expelled, and directors' assets were confiscated to compensate victims.
  • Underlying Logic:
    • "New Thing" Illusion: A renewed fascination with the "joint-stock company" form and false fantasies about South American trade profits.
    • Suppression of Dissent: Even Newton was not immune. He lamented that he could calculate the motion of celestial bodies but not "the madness of people."
    • External Scapegoating: The public only blamed the directors' fraud; Charles Mackay noted that "no one blamed the avarice of the nation."

American Tradition

Through an analysis of multiple fluctuations in 19th-century America, the author demonstrates that speculative euphoria was not accidental but a structural inertia accompanying the nation's growth.

1. Colonial Paper Money and Tobacco Notes

  • Factual Elements:
    • Asset: Tobacco warehouse receipts, Massachusetts paper money issued due to Sir William Phips' failed expedition to Quebec.
    • Central Figures: Sir William Phips.
    • Form of Leverage: Single-layer leverage. Issuing "promises" to redeem metal or tobacco in the future as currency, with actual hard currency reserves far below debt certificates.
    • Trigger Point: The failed expedition led to an empty treasury; subsequently, the British Parliament forcibly ordered a ban on issuance in 1751.
    • Post-crash Accountability: Colonial residents were angry at London's ban, refusing to reflect on the over-issuance.
  • Underlying Logic:
    • "New Thing" Illusion: Believing that "gold and silver are old, paper money is a magical innovation for creating wealth."
    • Suppression of Dissent: Support for currency creation at the time was seen as patriotic; Benjamin Franklin (as a paper money printer) also endorsed it.
    • External Scapegoating Narrative: Blaming the eventual worthlessness of paper money on the arrival of "reckoning day" or British intervention, rather than the initial frenzy.

2. The Panic of 1819

  • Factual Elements:
    • Asset: Public lands, real estate, and various assets after the War of 1812.
    • Central Figures: No specific individual dominated the outbreak of the panic; the Second Bank played a role in fueling this bubble.
    • Form of Leverage: State banks expanded wildly; barbers and bartenders competed to issue bank notes with almost no asset backing.
    • Trigger Point: The Second Bank of the United States began to tighten credit and demand redemption of notes.
    • Post-crash Accountability: A wave of bank failures; President Andrew Jackson, in response to public sentiment, revoked the Second Bank's charter.
  • Underlying Logic:
    • "New Thing" Illusion: Believing that lifting central bank regulation was an "innovation of credit freedom."
    • Suppression of Dissent: The Second Bank's regulatory actions were denounced as "elite oppression of the masses' opportunity to get rich."
    • External Scapegoating Narrative: Classic escapism. People blamed the Second Bank for tightening monetary policy as an "economic destroyer" rather than blaming speculation.

3. The Canal Bubble of 1837

  • Factual Elements:
    • Asset: Infrastructure projects like canals and toll roads, western public lands.
    • Central Figures: No specific individuals; rather, the credit endorsement of various state governments.
    • Form of Leverage: A large influx of British capital supported construction on a scale far exceeding actual demand through transatlantic borrowing.
    • Trigger Point: The inevitable collapse of confidence and the subsequent liquidation.
    • Post-crash Accountability: Collective default pattern. States like Mississippi and Louisiana directly declared their debts invalid.
  • Underlying Logic:
    • "New Thing" Illusion: Believing that large-scale transportation infrastructure could generate "infinite increments."
    • Suppression of Dissent: Demands for debt repayment were seen as an infringement on sovereignty, forming a collective consensus that "default is justice."
    • External Scapegoating Narrative: Blaming creditors. Declaring that British bankers were "foolish" to lend them money initially, and the consequences should be borne by the creditors themselves.

4. The Bank Panic of 1857

  • Factual Elements:
    • Asset: A large number of bank charters issued and bank notes (paper money) issued by state banks.
    • Central Figures: The core group consisted of bankers in Michigan and New England.
    • Form of Leverage: Extremely fraudulent reserve leverage. Banknote issuance far exceeded hard currency reserves; and auditors were deceived by placing nails at the bottom of gold coin boxes and multiple banks rotating the same batch of gold coins.
    • Trigger Point: Financial memory had just passed 20 years (since 1837), public confidence recovered to a blind degree, and the system spontaneously broke due to excessive leverage.
    • Post-crash Accountability: Many banks closed, and scrutiny of fraudulent reserves increased.
  • Underlying Logic:
    • "New Thing" Illusion: Believing in the "magic of money creation," thinking that continuous issuance of paper money could sustain economic growth, and treating this ancient debt creation as an innovative economic tool.
    • Suppression of Dissent: As the painful memory of 1837 faded, a new generation of speculators entered with "extreme confidence," dismissing past lessons as "primitive refuges," thereby rejecting rational risk assessment.
    • External Scapegoating Narrative: Academia began to "normalize" it, calling it part of a natural "business cycle," believing that the crash was merely the system "detoxing" or "restoring sanity," thus exempting speculative tendencies from moral scrutiny.

5. The Railroad Bubble of 1873

  • Factual Elements:
    • Asset: Railroad securities, railroad infrastructure investments.
    • Central Figures: Jay Cooke and his company, who was considered a financial authority at the time.
    • Form of Leverage: Huge British loans induced by financial amnesia; railroad construction relied not only on debt but also on serious inflated values and price manipulation.
    • Trigger Point: Jay Cooke's company went bankrupt in September 1873, and the NYSE was unusually closed for 10 days.
    • Post-crash Accountability: Widespread unemployment, bank failures, and depression.
  • Underlying Logic:
    • "New Thing" Illusion: Believing that railroads were an "inexhaustible source of gold," their development potential was touted as an unprecedented opportunity of the era.
    • Suppression of Dissent: Anyone questioning excessive railroad construction was seen as lacking foresight.
    • External Scapegoating Narrative: Blaming the monetary system. Instead of reflecting on railroad speculation, people blamed the "abolition of silver coins" or the "gold standard plan," giving rise to the Greenback Party and the Free Silver movement.

The Great Crash of 1929

The US stock market rose continuously in the 1920s, and investment trusts proliferated—investors bought shares in trust companies, which then borrowed money and issued bonds to buy shares in other companies, creating nested layers. A typical example was United Founders Corporation, which started with $500 in capital and eventually leveraged nearly $1 billion in assets. Retail investors could buy stocks with only a 10% margin. Even Irving Fisher, Yale University's most renowned economist, who was deeply involved in stock market speculation himself, publicly asserted in the autumn of 1929 that "stock prices have reached what looks like a permanently high plateau." The few voices warning of overheating (such as banker Paul Warburg) were fiercely attacked. On October 24 ("Black Thursday"), prices fell almost without limit; the heads of Morgan, Citi, and Chase banks met urgently to pool money to prop up the market, and prices briefly stabilized, but confidence quickly crumbled again, followed by several days of sharp declines, leading into a long depression.

  • Factual Elements:
    • Asset: Common stocks, nested investment trusts.
    • Central Figures: Charles Mitchell, Albert Wiggin, and the scholar Irving Fisher who endorsed them.
    • Form of Leverage: Extreme leverage. Margin trading with a very low 10% down payment, and multi-layered trust structures like Goldman Sachs and United Founders Corporation.
    • Trigger Point: An irreversible selling wave began in late October.
    • Post-crash Accountability: Charles Mitchell and Albert Wiggin were both immediately dismissed. Mitchell was prosecuted for alleged tax evasion; criminal charges were eventually acquitted, but he faced heavy civil damages; Wiggin's pension was revoked.
  • Underlying Logic:
    • "New Thing" Illusion: Believing that the Republican government's laissez-faire policy created a "permanently high level."
    • Suppression of Dissent: Warnings from Paul Warburg and Roger Babson were met with vicious ridicule and attacks.
    • External Scapegoating: Mainstream economists blamed external indicators like reduced production, attempting to protect the belief that "the market is inherently perfect."

After the crash, the SEC was established, holding companies were restricted, and margin was controlled—the author admits these measures were "not without value," but they did not touch the decisive factor of "people's inherent tendency to speculate."

The October 1987 Crisis

In the 1960s and 70s, the US had already experienced several smaller speculative rehearsals—"Go-Go boy" fund managers, Bernard Cornfeld's IOS "fund of funds" (essentially a sales pyramid with layered commissions, where most investor money was eaten by fees rather than truly invested), real estate investment trusts (REITs), and large banks recycling OPEC petrodollars to lend to Latin American countries (which later defaulted heavily). These rehearsals ended prematurely on a smaller scale, failing to prevent the larger-scale repetition in the 1980s. On October 19, 1987, after a bull market, the market plunged in a single day, known as "Black Monday." After the crash, the NYSE, SEC, and a presidential commission each conducted investigations. Most reports focused on technical triggers like program trading and portfolio insurance, with some even implying that regulation itself caused the crash, almost entirely omitting the inherent nature of speculation. Due to the safety net mechanisms established after 1929, such as unemployment benefits and deposit insurance, this crash had a much smaller impact on the real economy than 1929.

  • Factual Elements:
    • Asset: Corporate bonds, mergers and acquisitions supported by junk bonds.
    • Central Figures: Michael Milken, Ivan Boesky.
    • Form of Leverage: Large-scale leveraged buyouts (LBOs) through junk bonds.
    • Trigger Point: A single-day plunge on October 19 (Black Monday).
    • Post-crash Accountability: Milken went to prison, Drexel Burnham Lambert went bankrupt.
  • Underlying Logic:
    • "New Thing" Illusion: Junk bonds were seen as a great invention like the light bulb; Milken was given Edison-like status.
    • Suppression of Dissent: The author's warnings before the crash were ridiculed as "he just doesn't like to see other people make money."
    • External Scapegoating: Official reports (like the Brady Report) blamed "program trading" or "budget deficits," completely avoiding discussion of speculative nature.

My Thoughts

  • Distinction of Leverage: The book's "leverage" is a broad concept. In the case comparisons, I've broken it down into single-layer leverage (bounded risk) and circular/nested leverage (exponentially amplified risk). Circular leverage was already formed as early as John Law (1720), two centuries before the 1929 investment trusts, and is not a product of modern finance. Single-layer leverage is controllable, and its reasonable use is not an issue; when circular leverage appears, one needs to start being vigilant.
  • Essence of Trigger Points: Crash trigger points seem diverse (runs demanding gold redemption, margin calls that cannot be met), but the essence is the same thing—the total amount of book promises exceeds the real assets that can be redeemed. Once someone (whether creditor or debtor) first demands redemption at book value, the system collapses.
  • The "outcome obfuscation" stage is universally characterized by the inevitable avoidance of self-reflection on speculation itself after a crash, but the specific strategies for avoidance vary by national context and culture.
    • The US tends to default (in 1837, states directly refused to pay foreign debts, actively shifting responsibility, with an aggressive stance).
    • Britain during the South Sea Bubble tended to punish specific individuals (heavy penalties for directors, expulsion of MPs, completing avoidance by "finding bad guys").
    • Japan tends to bury its head in the sand (procrastination, fake restructuring, collective silence; see additional analysis below).
  • From the 1980s onwards, a series of so-called manias and crises largely show the shadow of the United States. It's hard to believe that there wasn't US planning/involvement behind them, used to shift domestic crises and contradictions and harvest wealth from other countries. Of course, these are just subjective conjectures, without any verification.
  • Unresolved Open Questions (not directly related to the book, reserved for future examination):
    • Is speculative euphoria a structural flaw unique to capitalism, or a universal phenomenon that appears in any scenario with resource competition—to be examined by Das Kapital.
    • Can "fully rational AI" managing economic activities eradicate euphoria: training data itself carries human irrationality, decision-making power is merely shifted one layer back (who sets the objective function), and centralized decision errors might cause greater damage than decentralized but mutually constrained markets—currently an open question, not a conclusion.

Additional Analysis

Japan's Bubble Economy (1985-1990s)

After the 1985 Plaza Accord, the yen appreciated sharply, threatening Japanese exports. The Bank of Japan drastically cut interest rates to offset the impact, maintaining them at a post-war low of 2.5% for over two years. Cheap credit poured into the stock market and real estate. Japanese companies generally favored "financial engineering"—borrowing low-interest money not for core business, but for stock and land speculation. Speculative gains were then counted as operating profit, making financial reports look better, which in turn supported stock prices and led to easier refinancing. Meanwhile, companies engaged in cross-shareholding, and banks lent against land as collateral, further pushing up land values, forming a double circular leverage. From 1985 to 1989, the Nikkei index tripled, at one point accounting for over a third of global stock market capitalization. Tokyo land prices were pushed to the point where they could "theoretically buy the entire United States," and some industry stocks were speculated to P/E ratios over 300 times. By the end of 1989, the central bank realized it had lost control and reversed course, continuously raising interest rates. In 1990, the Nikkei index halved. Afterward, Japanese banks and regulators chose to procrastinate—tacitly allowing non-performing loans not to be written off, and continuously injecting funds into insolvent companies through fake restructurings, creating a large number of "zombie companies." The problem dragged on until the collective bankruptcy of housing loan companies ("Jusen") in 1995-96 and the collapse of two major securities firms in late 1997 forced a showdown, plunging the Japanese economy into over a decade of stagnation.

  • Factual Elements:
    • Asset: Tokyo real estate and Nikkei index stocks.
    • Central Figures: The mythologized "Japanese management model," large corporate conglomerates, and omnipotent government regulatory agencies.
    • Form of Leverage: Double circular leverage. Using ultra-low interest credit, with constantly appreciating land as collateral to speculate in stocks; cross-shareholding and speculative gains were also counted as profits to support higher stock prices and obtain refinancing.
    • Trigger Point: By the end of 1989, the central bank realized it had lost control and continuously raised interest rates sharply.
    • Post-crash Accountability: Initially, regulatory agencies and banks chose "concealment and procrastination." There was even discussion at the time of modifying accounting standards to allow losing institutions to keep their losses secret, thereby avoiding reality. It wasn't until the collapse of large securities firms in 1997 that the true losses were forced into the open.
  • Underlying Logic:
    • "New Thing" Illusion: Believing in "Japanese-style capitalism" and the myth that "the Japanese market only goes up," thinking its management system had transcended traditional economic cycles.
    • Suppression of Dissent: The immense national wealth bought off the intelligence of participants. At the time, society generally believed that the insane valuation of the Nikkei index reflected Japan's extraordinary national strength, and questioning the bubble was dismissed as not understanding Japan's national conditions.
    • External Scapegoating Narrative: Systemic concealment. After the crash, regulatory agencies tried to keep institutional losses secret through technical means like modifying accounting standards, using administrative intervention to protect the "market perfection" totem, refusing a thorough liquidation and detox.

Mexico's "Tequila Crisis" (1994-1995)

Refers to the Mexican financial crisis that erupted from late 1994 to early 1995, named after the country's famous tequila.

  1. Background and Illusion: In January 1994, NAFTA (North American Free Trade Agreement) came into effect, and Mexico was seen as a model emerging market, attracting a large influx of foreign capital (mainly highly volatile portfolio investments).
  2. External Shocks and Political Instability: In 1994, the US Federal Reserve raised interest rates six times consecutively (from 3% to 5.5%), causing capital to flow back to the US. At the same time, Mexico experienced internal political violence, including the Zapatista uprising and the assassination of presidential candidate Colosio, severely damaging investor confidence.
  3. "Reinvention" of Leverage: To prevent capital flight, the Mexican government massively replaced peso-denominated national debt with "Tesobonos" bonds, which were pegged to the US dollar. By the end of 1994, the proportion of Tesobonos in foreign-held short-term debt soared from 6% at the beginning of the year to 87%, meaning the government effectively bore extremely high exchange rate risk.
  4. Crash Trigger: Facing reserve depletion, on December 20, the new government attempted to raise the exchange rate fluctuation ceiling by 15%, but this was seen by investors as a signal of default. Within two days, $5 billion fled, central bank reserves were exhausted, and Mexico was forced to announce a free-floating peso. Subsequently, the peso's exchange rate against the dollar nearly halved.
  5. International Bailout and Aftermath: In January 1995, US President Clinton bypassed Congress and, using the Exchange Stabilization Fund (ESF), teamed up with the IMF to provide an aid package of approximately $50 billion (of which the US contributed $20 billion). Mexico fell into a severe recession but eventually repaid the loans ahead of schedule in 1997.
  • Factual Elements:
    • The Artifact: Mexican government bonds (especially dollar-denominated Tesobonos) and emerging market securities.
    • The Presumed Genius: The Salinas government's "technocratic" team (mostly US-educated elites, once seen by international finance as geniuses bringing Mexico into modernity).
    • The Leverage: Exchange rate risk transfer leverage. By issuing dollarized short-term debt (Tesobonos), the government implicitly used national credit to guarantee investors' exchange rate risk, thereby propping up fragile capital inflows. This leverage would have a devastating amplifying effect when the peso depreciated.
    • The Start and Trigger of the Crash: The assassination of Colosio in March 1994 triggered the first round of reserve depletion; the clumsy "15% devaluation" announcement on December 20 triggered the final panic run.
    • Post-crash Recrimination: Finance Minister Serra Puche resigned; internationally, there was fierce debate over whether the US was using taxpayers' money to bail out "Wall Street speculators" and "mismanaged governments," leading to long-term discussions about "moral hazard."
  • Underlying Logic:
    • "New Thing" Illusion: The prevailing illusion at the time was that "Mexico had 'arrived' (modernization)." People believed that NAFTA and technocratic reforms had created an "unprecedented" new environment, making traditional current account deficits no longer a risk. This optimism masked the fragility of the short-term foreign debt structure.
    • Suppression of Dissent: The outgoing President Salinas, to maintain an "unblemished" political image (including campaigning for WTO head), severely suppressed any devaluation suggestions. Voices warning that the peso was overvalued were dismissed as "destroying prosperity," and the government forcibly maintained the market's collective illusion by repeatedly promising "no devaluation."
    • External Scapegoating: After the crash, official and mainstream views tended to blame "acts of God" (political assassinations, uprisings) and "external environment" (Fed interest rate hikes), thereby avoiding reflection on the speculative essence of "over-reliance on short-term dollarized debt."

This bailout was defined as resolving a "liquidity crisis" rather than a "structural speculative tendency." The bailout put out the fire, but the inherent mechanism of leverage was preserved in the wave of financial globalization, and the same pattern repeated itself in the Asian Financial Crisis two years later.

Asian Financial Crisis (1997-1998)

From 1985-1996, Thailand's economy grew at an average annual rate of over 9%, becoming one of the fastest-growing economies globally. The Thai baht was pegged to the US dollar, and its stable exchange rate attracted a large influx of foreign capital. Thai financial companies borrowed heavily in short-term foreign currencies like US dollars and then issued long-term loans in Thai baht, primarily investing in non-productive sectors like real estate—this created a double mismatch of currency and maturity: borrowing short-term US dollars and lending long-term Thai baht. Real estate and stock markets subsequently overheated. By 1996, Thailand's current account deficit expanded to 8% of GDP, and the baht was widely considered overvalued. In early 1997, hedge funds led by George Soros began to heavily short the Thai baht. The Bank of Thailand used billions of dollars in foreign exchange reserves to defend the currency, but reserves were quickly depleted. On July 2, Thailand was forced to abandon its peg, and the baht depreciated sharply, reaching its bottom in early 1998. The Thai stock market fell by 75%, and Finance One, one of the largest financial companies, collapsed.

The crisis quickly spread to Indonesia, Malaysia, South Korea, and the Philippines—companies in these countries also commonly borrowed in US dollars and lent or operated in local currencies. When local currencies depreciated, the local currency value of US dollar debts instantly soared, leading to widespread corporate insolvency. The Indonesian rupiah depreciated the most, with over 60% of bank loans becoming non-performing. This crisis directly led to Japan's severe recession in 1997-1998 and triggered the collapse of major Japanese financial institutions (such as Yamaichi Securities). The IMF provided over $100 billion in aid to Thailand, Indonesia, and South Korea, but the attached austerity conditions (interest rate hikes, fiscal spending cuts, bank closures) were heavily criticized at the time for exacerbating rather than alleviating the crisis. Economists like Joseph Stiglitz later publicly criticized the IMF's handling; at the same time, "crony capitalism" became the most prevalent narrative for blame—attributing the crisis to the Asian countries' own governance corruption and cronyism, rather than the systemic risks of international capital free flow and short-term foreign debt structures themselves.

  • Factual Elements:
    • Asset: Thai baht and other Southeast Asian currencies (Indonesian rupiah, Malaysian ringgit, Philippine peso), real estate and stock market assets reliant on short-term foreign debt.
    • Central Figures: No single "financial genius" individual played this role; instead, the entire growth myth of the "East Asian Miracle" / "Asian Tigers" played a similar role at the national/regional level—economies like Thailand were widely seen as flawless models of growth until the crash, with almost no one questioning them.
    • Form of Leverage: Double mismatch of currency and maturity. Thai financial companies borrowed heavily in short-term US dollar loans, then issued long-term Thai baht loans, primarily investing in non-productive sectors like real estate—borrowing short-term US dollars and lending long-term local currency. Once the local currency depreciated, the repayment pressure of US dollar debts instantly amplified.
    • Trigger Point: Hedge funds led by George Soros launched a massive short-selling attack on the Thai baht. The Bank of Thailand used billions of dollars in foreign exchange reserves to defend the currency, but reserves were exhausted. On July 2, 1997, it was forced to abandon its peg, and the baht depreciated sharply, with the crisis then spreading to Indonesia, Malaysia, South Korea, and the Philippines.
    • Post-crash Accountability: The IMF and mainstream public opinion quickly attributed the crisis to "crony capitalism"—blaming Asian countries' own governance corruption and political-business collusion, rather than systemic risks like international capital free flow and short-term foreign debt structures. Economists like Joseph Stiglitz later publicly criticized this narrative, arguing that the IMF's austerity prescriptions (interest rate hikes, fiscal spending cuts, bank closures) exacerbated the crisis rather than alleviating it.
  • Underlying Logic:
    • "New Thing" Illusion: Not entirely that a specific financial instrument was packaged as a "new thing," but rather that the entire growth model itself was mythologized—from 1985 to 1996, Thailand's annual growth exceeded 9%, and the "Asian Economic Miracle" was widely seen as a new, sustainable development paradigm. The stability brought by fixed exchange rates was treated as free insurance, rather than being seen as masking real exchange rate risk exposure.
    • Suppression of Dissent: Warnings did exist but were marginalized—economist Paul Krugman published an article in 1994 questioning whether the "Asian Miracle" growth was mainly due to capital and labor input accumulation rather than real productivity improvement. This article was widely ignored or even ridiculed at the time, only to be revisited after the crisis erupted. This mirrors the author's own experience of having an article rejected in 1987.
    • External Scapegoating: The "crony capitalism" blame narrative itself is a typical form of external scapegoating—precisely locating responsibility in "Asian countries' cultural and governance flaws," thereby avoiding a more fundamental and embarrassing question for Wall Street and international capital markets: Does the international capital flow system itself have structural flaws in allowing short-term hot money to flow in and out of emerging markets freely and frictionlessly? This case can be said to be the most blatant instance of "external scapegoating" in the entire book—responsibility was completely transferred to the victim.

Russian Debt Default and LTCM Collapse (1998)

In 1994, John Meriwether, a former star bond trader at Salomon Brothers, founded the hedge fund LTCM. The team brought together top traders and two Nobel laureates in economics, Myron Scholes and Robert Merton. Using quantitative models to capture tiny price differences between various fixed-income assets for arbitrage, and relying on the model's "precision," they leveraged less than $1 billion of their own capital to control over $100 billion in assets, with leverage once approaching 100 times. On August 17, 1998, the Russian government devalued the ruble and defaulted on its domestic debt, triggering an extreme global flight to safety. LTCM's model assumptions of narrowing spreads and low correlations all failed; almost all asset correlations instantly converged to 1. In August alone, losses reached $1.85 billion, accounting for 45% of remaining capital. By September, capital had shrunk to about $600 million, while the balance sheet still exceeded $100 billion, and the leverage ratio soared to about 167:1. The New York Fed stepped in, leading 14 banks to inject $3.6 billion to take over LTCM, preventing a disorderly collapse from triggering wider panic.

Factual Elements:

  • Asset: Fixed-income arbitrage positions (government bond spreads, swap spreads, mortgage-backed securities, etc.)
  • Central Figures: John Meriwether and the team's two Nobel laureates in economics
  • Form of Leverage: Extreme leverage, peaking near 100 times, soaring to about 167:1 at the time of the collapse
  • Trigger Point: Russia's default triggered a global "flight to safety," with spreads and correlations reversing simultaneously
  • Post-crash Accountability: The New York Fed led private banks to inject capital and take over, sparking debate about the moral hazard of "should it be saved," with no strong narrative of punishing specific individuals

Underlying Logic:

  • "New Thing" Illusion: The "new" this time was not a specific financial instrument, but the belief itself that "quantitative models can eliminate risk"—arbitrage trading using Nobel-prize-level mathematical models was seen as transforming speculation into a precise science. This confidence that "risk has been calculated and hedged" is essentially the same psychology as Fisher's 1929 confidence that "stock prices have reached a permanently high plateau," only this time packaged in the language of mathematical finance.
  • Suppression of Dissent: It wasn't entirely that external skeptics were suppressed, but rather that the model itself internally suppressed doubts about its own assumptions—low correlation and converging spreads were treated as mathematical facts rather than judgments requiring verification. The academic authority of the team (two Nobel laureates) itself constituted an implicit suppression of "who dares question a Nobel laureate's model."
  • External Scapegoating: Responsibility was attributed to "the unprecedented black swan event of Russia's default," rather than to the decision itself of "using nearly 100 times leverage for arbitrage trading believed to be foolproof."

This "new thing illusion" and "external scapegoating" actually formed a closed loop: first, using the model's precision to convince oneself that leverage was safe, and after the incident, blaming external shocks that the model couldn't foresee, rather than reflecting on whether "betting on the model's correctness with extreme leverage" itself was problematic.

Dot-com Bubble (1997-2002)

With the commercialization of the internet in 1995, the Nasdaq Composite Index rose from 751 points in January 1995 to a peak of 5048 points on March 10, 2000, a cumulative increase of nearly 600%. A large number of internet startups went public without stable business models or even revenue; in 1999, 39% of total venture capital flowed to internet companies. A company called theglobe.com saw its stock price surge over 600% on its IPO day, setting a record for first-day gains on Wall Street at the time. By March 2000, 74% of surveyed internet companies had negative cash flow, and only 14% of new tech stocks in the Nasdaq 100 index were actually profitable. Cisco's price-to-sales ratio was once speculated to around 200 times (mature companies typically range from 1-5 times). Star Wall Street analysts (such as Henry Blodget of Merrill Lynch and Jack Grubman of Salomon Smith Barney) continuously publicly touted internet stocks, helping investment banks secure underwriting business for these companies. In late 1999, the Federal Reserve raised interest rates consecutively to curb an overheating economy, reaching 5.75% in February 2000 and warning of further tightening. On March 13, 2000, news of Japan's renewed economic recession triggered a global sell-off, and Nasdaq began to fall; on March 20, Barron's published an article warning that several internet companies were running out of cash, intensifying the panic; in April, Nasdaq experienced its worst week ever, plunging over 25% in a single week. The bear market continued for the next two and a half years, and by October 4, 2002, Nasdaq had fallen 78% from its peak, evaporating over $5 trillion in market value. Famous companies like Pets.com, Webvan, and eToys successively went bankrupt and liquidated.

  • Factual Elements:
    • Asset: Internet and tech company stocks, especially newly listed companies without a clear profit model.
    • Central Figures: Star Wall Street analysts Henry Blodget (Merrill Lynch) and Jack Grubman (Salomon Smith Barney), who continuously published bullish research reports, helping their investment banks secure underwriting business for these companies.
    • Form of Leverage: Not entirely reliant on traditional lending leverage, but the venture capital and IPO market itself constituted leverage—a large amount of capital flowed to companies without profitability or even revenue, with valuations entirely based on an unverified narrative of "future potential."
    • Trigger Point: News of Japan's economic recession in March 2000 triggered a global tech stock sell-off, compounded by Barron's report warning of cash depletion at several internet companies.
    • Post-crash Accountability: In 2002-2003, New York Attorney General Eliot Spitzer released Merrill Lynch's internal emails, showing Blodget privately calling some stocks "junk" while publicly issuing bullish reports. Grubman, Blodget, and other analysts, along with over a dozen investment banks, eventually reached settlements totaling nearly $900 million with the SEC. Grubman and Blodget were both permanently banned from the securities industry.
  • Underlying Logic:
    • "New Thing" Illusion: The internet was seen as a "new economy" fundamentally changing business rules. Traditional valuation metrics like profitability and cash flow were considered obsolete; "eyeball economy" (user growth rather than profit) became the new valuation logic. This was one of the purest manifestations of the "new thing illusion"—even the measurement standards themselves were "innovated."
    • Suppression of Dissent: The detail of analysts privately bearish but publicly bullish transformed "suppression of dissent" from "external skeptics being suppressed" into analysts self-suppressing their true judgment. Blodget and others were not unaware of the risks, but public optimistic statements directly related to the investment bank's underwriting revenue. This was a commercially structured self-censorship, more systemic than simply "not daring to speak the truth."
    • External Scapegoating: Post-crash accountability focused on a few specific analysts (Grubman, Blodget), using huge fines and permanent bans to complete the "punish specific individuals" pattern. However, the more fundamental issue of "analyst compensation linked to investment banking underwriting business," which created conflicts of interest, was not truly addressed, although some reforms were made (requiring analyst compensation to be decoupled from investment banking). The valuation preference of venture capital and public markets for "storytelling over profit" also remained largely untouched.

2007-2008 Global Financial Crisis (Subprime Mortgage Crisis)

After the dot-com bubble burst in 2000, the Federal Reserve sharply cut interest rates to stimulate the economy, and cheap credit flowed into the housing market. Mortgage lenders issued a large number of "subprime loans" (i.e., borrowers with poor credit and questionable repayment ability could still obtain mortgages); by 2006, one-third of new mortgages in the US were subprime or "no-doc" loans. These mortgages were packaged into "mortgage-backed securities" (MBS), and then re-sliced and recombined into "collateralized debt obligations" (CDO). Rating agencies gave these securities extremely high credit ratings, despite the underlying assets being full of risk. In 2004, the five major investment banks (Goldman Sachs, Lehman, Merrill Lynch, Bear Stearns, Morgan Stanley) reached an agreement with the SEC, receiving exemptions from traditional capital adequacy limits, further amplifying leverage. Housing prices peaked in 2006, and subprime loan default rates soared in 2007, causing the value of MBS and CDOs to collapse. In March 2008, Bear Stearns was on the verge of bankruptcy and was acquired by JPMorgan Chase at a low price; on September 15, Lehman Brothers declared bankruptcy without government bailout, the largest bankruptcy in US history, and the Dow Jones Industrial Average plunged over 500 points in response; the next day, American International Group (AIG), the world's largest insurance company, was on the verge of collapse due to extensive sales of credit default swaps (CDS) to "insure" CDOs, and was taken over by the government. Panic quickly spread throughout the global financial system. Congress subsequently passed the $700 billion "Troubled Asset Relief Program" (TARP), and the Federal Reserve and Treasury Department jointly bailed out several "too big to fail" financial institutions. The crisis ultimately evolved into the most severe global economic recession since the Great Depression.

  • Factual Elements:
    • Asset: Subprime mortgages and their securitized products (MBS, CDO), and credit default swaps (CDS) that "insured" them.
    • Central Figures: No single "financial genius"; instead, the entire Wall Street investment banking system (Goldman Sachs, Lehman, Bear Stearns, etc.), along with rating agencies and mortgage lenders, collectively drove this euphoria.
    • Form of Leverage: Multi-layered nested securitization—mortgages themselves were leverage, packaged into MBS was the first layer of leverage amplification, MBS then re-sliced and recombined into CDOs was the second layer, and some institutions also used CDS to further leverage/speculate on CDOs, forming the most structurally complex multi-layered nested leverage in the entire book.
    • Trigger Point: Housing prices stopped rising in 2007, subprime default rates soared, and Lehman Brothers' bankruptcy in September 2008 became the tipping point that broke market confidence.
    • Post-crash Accountability: This was the weakest "post-crash accountability" in the case studies—compared to the thousands of executives prosecuted and imprisoned during the 1980s S&L crisis, almost no senior executives of major Wall Street investment banks faced criminal charges after the 2008 crisis. Not a single executive from Lehman, Citi, Bear Stearns, or AIG was criminally prosecuted; the only one sentenced to prison was a relatively peripheral loan officer.
  • Underlying Logic:
    • "New Thing" Illusion: Securitization and credit default swaps were packaged as "financial innovations that diversify risk." Rating agencies gave these complex products extremely high ratings. This was essentially taking the author's iron rule that "financial operations have no true innovation" and packaging it to the extreme with the most complex mathematical models and the longest securitization chains.
    • Suppression of Dissent: A few people who discovered the subprime problem early and shorted the market (e.g., hedge fund manager Michael Burry) were long ridiculed by peers as not understanding the market before the crash. At the same time, rating agencies giving high ratings to toxic assets and investment banks continuing to package and sell underlying assets they knew were problematic constituted a systemic "silent complicity" rather than mere individual optimism.
    • External Scapegoating: This "external scapegoating" took an extreme variant—responsibility was not transferred to a specific scapegoat, but almost no one was truly held accountable. This indicates that when leverage structures are complex enough and responsibility is sufficiently dispersed across every part of the system, even the most basic avoidance mechanism of "blaming individuals" can fail, ultimately leading to a bailout of the entire system rather than a liquidation of any specific actions.

European Sovereign Debt Crisis (2010-2012)

In 2001, to meet the fiscal deficit criteria required for joining the Eurozone, Greece entered into a "currency swap" deal with Goldman Sachs—using a deliberately set fictitious exchange rate to convert some of Greece's foreign currency debt into €2.8 billion that "disappeared" from the books, helping Greece's deficit figures appear to meet the Eurozone threshold. Goldman Sachs received payment for this. Such accounting tricks were not uncommon among EU countries at the time; Italy also used similar operations to hide debt. Greece thus successfully joined the Eurozone, and for years afterward, continued to use similar methods to maintain its deficit figures below the surface target of 3%, until a new government came to power in 2009 and admitted that the previous government had significantly understated the true fiscal deficit and debt levels—the actual deficit was close to 12% of GDP, far higher than previously announced figures. In April 2010, rating agencies downgraded Greek government bonds to junk status, and private capital markets immediately closed their doors to Greece. On May 2, the EU, European Central Bank, and IMF ("Troika") jointly provided €110 billion in bailout loans, with strict austerity conditions—drastic cuts in public spending, tax increases, and pension reductions—triggering years of large-scale protests and riots in Greece. The crisis quickly spread to Ireland (November 2010), Portugal (May 2011), Spain, and Cyprus (2012), collectively known as "PIIGS." Greece subsequently received additional aid twice more in 2012 and 2015. A large portion of the cumulative bailout loans was actually used to repay European banks that had previously lent funds, rather than truly for Greece's own economic reconstruction.

  • Factual Elements:
    • Asset: Eurozone member sovereign bonds, especially those of Greece, Ireland, Portugal, Spain.
    • Central Figures: No single individual; instead, successive Greek governments (systematically understating deficit data for a long time) and investment banks like Goldman Sachs (providing "financial engineering" to help conceal true liabilities) collectively created this crisis.
    • Form of Leverage: Sovereign debt itself, plus using financial engineering to conceal the true scale of debt—this was not entirely "borrowing money to buy speculative items" in the traditional sense of leverage, but rather systematically making actual liabilities appear smaller than their true level on the books. This is essentially the same logic as John Law's "banknote issuance far exceeding coin reserves," only this time what was concealed was not the ratio of paper money to gold, but the ratio of a sovereign nation's true deficit to its reported deficit.
    • Trigger Point: In 2009, the new Greek government admitted that the previous government had significantly understated deficit data. Rating agencies immediately downgraded Greece's sovereign credit rating, triggering panic selling of Greek government bonds.
    • Post-crash Accountability: Responsibility was highly concentrated and attributed to Greece's "government corruption and lax fiscal discipline." Austerity measures targeted ordinary Greek citizens (pension cuts, public sector wage reductions), while Goldman Sachs and other investment banks that helped Greece conceal debt, and the EU accounting rules that tacitly allowed these practices for years, were largely not held accountable.
  • Underlying Logic:
    • "New Thing" Illusion: The unified Eurozone currency itself was seen as an "institutional innovation" that would end sovereign debt risk for member states—the market once believed that Greek and German government bonds should have similar risk, allowing Greece to borrow at low rates similar to Germany for years. This illusion itself was a core mechanism driving debt accumulation.
    • Suppression of Dissent: There were indeed doubts within the EU—IMF Executive Board members from countries like India, Brazil, and Argentina had warned in internal memos that Greek debt was unsustainable, but this was overruled by representatives from France, Germany, and the Netherlands. These countries were unwilling to let their private banks bear the cost of Greek debt write-downs, and dissenting voices were suppressed within the decision-making body rather than publicly debated.
    • External Scapegoating: This "external scapegoating" showed a clear inter-state transfer—responsibility was entirely placed on Greece's "political corruption and crony capitalism" (a narrative strikingly similar to "crony capitalism" after the Asian Financial Crisis), while the actual flow of bailout funds (mostly used to repay European banks' claims) and the role of investment banks in assisting debt concealment largely did not enter the scope of public accountability.

Cryptocurrency First Bubble (2017-2018)

Bitcoin started at around $1,000 in early 2017, surpassed $2,000 mid-year, reached $5,000 in September, then accelerated, breaking $10,000 by late November, and hitting a peak of $19,783 on December 17, a nearly 20-fold increase in a year. What truly made this euphoria massive was not just Bitcoin itself, but "Initial Coin Offering" (ICO), a new fundraising method—project teams could raise funds publicly with just a "whitepaper," without traditional securities regulatory approval. In 2017, the total cryptocurrency market capitalization surged from $16 billion to a peak of $535 billion, an increase of over 3200%. Thousands of new tokens emerged, mutually boosted by "Fear Of Missing Out" (FOMO) sentiment, forming a self-reinforcing cycle where "one token's rise creates a reason for the next token's rise." SoftBank founder Masayoshi Son reportedly lost $130 million personally in this bubble. In January 2018, the market sharply reversed; Bitcoin plunged about 65% in a month, and other "altcoins" generally fell even more. Subsequent research showed that about 78% of ICO projects that year were identified as pure scams. By the end of 2019, most ICO-issued tokens had lost over 90% of their value, and the cryptocurrency market collectively evaporated over $300 billion in market capitalization that year.

  • Factual Elements:
    • Asset: Bitcoin and thousands of newly issued "altcoins," especially project tokens raised through ICOs.
    • Central Figures: No single core figure; instead, tens of thousands of anonymous or semi-anonymous ICO project initiators collectively drove this euphoria. This was the most numerous and dispersed group of "coin creators" in all the case studies in the book.
    • Form of Leverage: Not entirely reliant on traditional lending leverage, but "zero-threshold certificate issuance" itself constituted leverage—anyone could create tradable tokens out of thin air and raise funds publicly with just a whitepaper. This was essentially the "issuing certificates for real funds" trick simplified to its extreme.
    • Trigger Point: In early 2018, a combination of tightening regulations in multiple countries, exchange hacks, and some platforms banning cryptocurrency advertisements led to a collapse in market confidence, triggering a chain sell-off.
    • Post-crash Accountability: There was no clear narrative of "punishing specific individuals"—since most project initiators were anonymous or outside regulatory jurisdiction, accountability was largely impossible, with only a very few extreme cases (e.g., projects later retroactively deemed fraudulent) being prosecuted.
  • Underlying Logic:
    • "New Thing" Illusion: Blockchain technology itself was indeed new, but the ICO fundraising model was packaged as "the next internet-level wealth opportunity everyone can participate in." This "newness" had both a real technological core and was over-extended to almost any project labeled "blockchain."
    • Suppression of Dissent: Due to the highly dispersed participants and lack of a unified authoritative body or individual to question or be questioned, "suppression of dissent" was relatively weak this time—skeptical voices (e.g., economists warning about the ICO bubble) did exist and were widely reported, but were more often drowned out by the collective volume of FOMO sentiment, rather than deliberately suppressed by specific vested interests.
    • External Scapegoating: Post-crash explanations were highly technical and depersonalized—blaming external technical factors like "tightening regulations," "exchange hacks," or "market manipulation." The core structural issue of "anyone can issue certificates with zero threshold to exchange for real funds" largely did not become a focus of public discussion.

2022 Cryptocurrency Crash (Terra/Luna, Three Arrows Capital, Celsius, FTX)

In early 2022, the cryptocurrency market was still at a high, with mainstream coins like Bitcoin having seen huge gains in the previous year, even appearing in Super Bowl ads. The crisis began with the Terra ecosystem—its algorithmic stablecoin UST claimed to maintain a 1:1 peg with the US dollar through a bidirectional exchange mechanism with its token Luna, without needing real US dollar reserves. The primary demand for UST came from a lending platform called Anchor Protocol, which offered users depositing UST an annualized yield of about 20%. This yield, far exceeding normal market levels, continuously attracted large amounts of capital, and UST's market capitalization once approached $19 billion. In May 2022, UST de-pegged, its price began to fall below $1. To maintain the peg, the system was forced to massively mint Luna to absorb selling pressure, resulting in an uncontrolled surge in Luna's supply, and its price subsequently collapsed to near zero. UST and Luna combined evaporated about $48 billion in market capitalization. This collapse quickly spread throughout the crypto industry: hedge fund Three Arrows Capital (3AC) became insolvent due to large holdings of Luna-related positions and high-leverage borrowing, filing for bankruptcy in June; crypto lending platform Celsius, facing similar liquidity crises, froze user withdrawals in June and declared bankruptcy in July, with a significant portion of its liabilities being user deposits; lending platforms like Voyager Digital and BlockFi were successively affected, temporarily sustained by credit lines from crypto exchange FTX. After the crisis temporarily subsided, in November, FTX was exposed for misappropriating user funds to its affiliated quantitative trading firm Alameda Research for high-risk investments and to cover losses. News of this triggered a run, with users withdrawing about $6 billion in 72 hours. FTX's capital chain broke, and it filed for bankruptcy on November 11. Founder Sam Bankman-Fried was subsequently charged with multiple counts of fraud.

  • Factual Elements:
    • Asset: Algorithmic stablecoin UST and its paired token Luna, customer deposits at crypto exchanges represented by FTX.
    • Central Figures: Terra founder Do Kwon, FTX founder Sam Bankman-Fried—the latter was once called "the J.P. Morgan of crypto" by mainstream media and was a recognized industry spokesperson.
    • Form of Leverage: Two different leverage structures—Terra was a circular leverage formed by the "algorithmic stable mechanism" itself (UST and Luna mutually backing each other, without real US dollar reserves). FTX, on the other hand, involved the more primitive capital mismatch of misappropriating customer deposits to prop up an affiliated company, essentially "on paper, it's customer assets, but in reality, it's been used elsewhere," closer to a direct financial black hole than circular leverage.
    • Trigger Point: UST de-pegging triggered a run-like sell-off, followed by the exposure of FTX's customer fund shortfall triggering a run.
    • Post-crash Accountability: Bankman-Fried was criminally charged and ultimately received a felony conviction, a rare outcome of clear criminal accountability for a specific individual. Do Kwon, meanwhile, was a long-term fugitive, later wanted by multiple countries, and eventually arrested.
  • Underlying Logic:
    • "New Thing" Illusion: Algorithmic stablecoins were packaged as a "financial innovation that can maintain price stability without real asset reserves." The 20% "risk-free" annualized yield was presented as proof of technological breakthrough, rather than being identified as an unsustainable subsidy.
    • Suppression of Dissent: Before the crash, some analysts did question the unsustainability of UST's reserve mechanism, but such doubts were long labeled as "not understanding the technology" or "bearish equals enemy" within the crypto community and marginalized. Bankman-Fried himself was long seen by mainstream media and regulators as a "responsible industry spokesperson" before the crash, further lowering external vigilance.
    • External Scapegoating: Due to the act of misappropriating customer funds itself constituting clear criminal fraud, responsibility fell relatively clearly on Sam Bankman-Fried personally. However, structural issues like "algorithmic stablecoins don't need real reserves" and "crypto lending platforms generally lack transparency" did not lead to systemic regulatory reforms after the crash. Accountability largely stopped at judicial proceedings against individual fraudsters.

So When Will This AI Bubble Burst (November 2022~)

Starting Point: ChatGPT Ignites Public Awareness (November 2022 - January 2023)

On November 30, 2022, OpenAI released ChatGPT for free as a "research preview." Without any large-scale marketing, it reached over 1 million users in 5 days and over 100 million users in 60 days—making it the fastest-growing consumer application in internet history at the time, 9 months faster than TikTok and over two years faster than Instagram. Google internally declared a "code red," fearing ChatGPT would threaten its core search advertising business. In January 2023, Microsoft announced an additional investment in OpenAI, bringing its total investment to approximately $13 billion, and securing about 49% of OpenAI's profits until its investment is recouped—this investment occurred against a somewhat ironic backdrop: just one week prior, Microsoft had announced layoffs of 10,000 employees. During the same period, Anthropic, founded by former OpenAI executives, also completed several hundred million dollars in funding in Q1 2023, kicking off the AI startup funding frenzy.

The Market's True "Big Bang": Nvidia's Performance Miracle (May 2023)

Most analysts believe that the tipping point where the AI craze truly transformed into a financial market phenomenon was not the release of ChatGPT, but Nvidia's quarterly earnings report on May 24, 2023—revenue guidance far exceeded market expectations by about 50%, stock price surged 24% in a single day, and market capitalization approached $1 trillion overnight. JPMorgan Chase immediately doubled its target price from $250 to $500 that day. Subsequently, Nvidia's performance continued to far exceed expectations for several consecutive quarters: Q2 revenue grew 101% year-over-year, net profit surged over 800%; Q3 revenue tripled, net profit grew over 13 times. For the full year 2023, Nvidia's stock price rose 239%, and its market capitalization surpassed $1 trillion mid-year, exceeding Amazon and Google's parent company Alphabet.

2024: Competition Fully Unfolds, Circular Funding Begins to Emerge

In 2024, Nvidia's market capitalization grew from $1 trillion to $2 trillion in 9 months (February 2024), and then to $3 trillion in another 3 months (June 2024). In November of the same year, Nvidia's latest generation Blackwell chip was reported to have pre-orders extending 12 months into the future. Meanwhile, Microsoft, Amazon, and Google began to intensively inject capital into AI labs like OpenAI and Anthropic, with conditions usually including commercial ties like "must use the investor's cloud services"—the embryonic form of circular funding largely took shape this year.

January 2025: The First Real Shock – The DeepSeek Moment

In January 2025, Chinese AI startup "DeepSeek" released its DeepSeek model, which was proven to achieve comparable performance to its Western counterparts at a significantly lower training cost. On the day the news broke, global tech stocks evaporated nearly $1 trillion in market value in a single day. Nvidia plunged 17% in one day (approximately $600 billion in market value evaporated, setting a record for single-day market value loss in US stocks at the time), but rebounded 8.8% the next day. This was the only market event close to a "crash rehearsal" in the entire AI craze to date, but it was quickly absorbed by the market—in the same month, OpenAI, SoftBank, and Oracle jointly announced the "Stargate" project, pledging up to $500 billion to build data center infrastructure. The sheer scale of this, on the contrary, further stimulated market sentiment.

Full Year 2025: Euphoria Spreads, Doubts Begin to Emerge

In 2025, Nvidia's market capitalization surpassed $4 trillion in July and $5 trillion in October—exceeding the GDP of any country except the US and China. AI-related companies contributed approximately 80% of the S&P 500's gains for the year. This year, the circular funding structure became dramatically more complex: Nvidia pledged up to $100 billion in investment to OpenAI, OpenAI pledged to purchase $250 billion in cloud services from Microsoft, OpenAI acquired a 10% stake in AMD while placing billions of dollars in chip orders with them, and Amazon and Google invested up to $4 billion and $2 billion respectively in Anthropic. A noteworthy detail: OpenAI founder Sam Altman himself publicly admitted this year, "I think there is indeed a bubble"—this is extremely rare in all the cases in the book: a core participant in the euphoria actively admitting its existence, yet market sentiment did not cool down.

2026 to Present: Scale Continues to Expand, Disagreements Become Public

Entering 2026, capital expenditure forecasts were repeatedly revised upwards—an initial forecast of approximately $405 billion at the beginning of the year was revised to $660-725 billion by mid-year, with Goldman Sachs predicting it would exceed $1 trillion in 2027. In February, Moody's disclosed that major tech companies' off-balance-sheet data center leasing commitments amounted to $662 billion, exceeding these companies' total on-balance-sheet debt. In June, Alphabet announced an $80 billion equity offering for AI infrastructure spending, seen as a signal that "own cash flow is no longer sufficient to support capital expenditures." In the same month, the 5-year credit default swap (CDS) spreads for Oracle and Microsoft had doubled compared to September, reflecting rising concerns in the bond market about their credit risk. Meanwhile, "Big Short" protagonist Michael Burry continuously increased his short positions (Nvidia, Tesla, Applied Materials, Caterpillar, Philadelphia Semiconductor Index) starting in May, and specifically likened the situation to "the last few months of the 2000 dot-com bubble." Internal corporate pushback also began to emerge—companies like Uber started limiting internal AI usage due to surging AI tool bills. At the same time, Chinese open-source models (DeepSeek V4 Pro, Zhipu GLM-5.2) had achieved comparable performance to top Western closed-source models at about one-tenth the cost. May data showed that over 60% of token consumption on the OpenRouter platform flowed to Chinese open-source models. Some interpreted this as evidence that "model layer profits are being compressed," but others argued it confirmed "Jevons' paradox"—that falling costs would only stimulate greater usage. As of now (July 2026), capital expenditure continues to accelerate, and no true crash has occurred. The market is in a state where bulls and bears, based on the same data, continuously contend over the future question of "can revenue catch up with expenditure."

  • Bears (with named individuals and institutions)
    • Michael Burry: Repeatedly publicly compared the current market to the peak of the 2000 dot-com bubble, warning that "circular funding" might be seen as a "scam rather than a virtuous cycle" in the future.
    • Economist Julien Garran (MacroStrategy Partnership): Called it "the biggest bubble in history."
    • Bridgewater Associates founder Ray Dalio: Directly likened it to the dot-com bubble.
    • Notably, OpenAI founder Sam Altman himself publicly admitted in 2025, "I think there is indeed a bubble."
  • Bulls (also with named individuals and institutions)
    • BlackRock CEO Larry Fink: Believes this capital investment is "worth it."
    • Nvidia CEO Jensen Huang: Believes efficiency gains will expand rather than shrink computing demand ("Jevons' paradox" logic).
    • Microsoft CEO Satya Nadella, Google CEO Sundar Pichai: Emphasize rapid growth in cloud AI revenue itself (Azure AI annualized revenue $37 billion, up 123% YoY; Google Cloud annualized $80 billion, up 63% YoY).
    • Oaktree Capital's Howard Marks: Believes valuations are "high but not yet crazy."
  • Consensus between both sides
    • Multiple studies indicate that both bulls and bears do not deny the same set of data (Nvidia's quarterly data center revenue of $75 billion, capital expenditure in the $700 billion range, AI sector contributing nearly 80% of S&P 500 gains). The disagreement lies only in two judgments: "Can this revenue growth catch up with the scale of expenditure?" and "How long can the market maintain patience with this uncertainty?" Both of these judgments point to the future, and neither side can confirm or deny them in the present.

Analysis and Forecast

  • Factual Elements:
    • Asset: Ostensibly AI-related company stocks and bonds, but essentially the unfulfilled expectation of "whether future AI revenue can catch up with today's capital expenditure."
    • Central Figures: No single individual; leading tech companies (Microsoft, Nvidia, Amazon, Google, Oracle) and leading AI companies (OpenAI, Anthropic) act as mutual investors, suppliers, and customers, collectively forming the driving force.
    • Form of Leverage: Dual nesting of equity cross-holdings and customer contract relationships—the same funds are repeatedly counted in both "investment" and "revenue" ledgers, and require continuous bond issuance to sustain. Oracle is currently the specific node recognized by the credit market as most vulnerable.
    • Trigger Point: Not yet known, still in the projection stage.
    • Post-crash Accountability: Not yet occurred, impossible to judge.
  • Underlying Logic:
    • "New Thing" Illusion: The underlying technology (AI) itself is a real new thing, but the financial mechanisms supporting it (equity investment, circular shareholding, bond issuance) are entirely old tricks—the novelty of the technology and the novelty of the financial structure need to be verified separately. This is a new variant that this case brings to the framework.
    • Suppression of Dissent: Skeptical voices (Burry, Dalio, etc.) exist publicly and are not suppressed, but they manifest in a new form—not suppressing the "right to speak," but rather the continuously rising prices themselves erode the "effectiveness of speaking," making skeptics appear "always wrong" and thus marginalized by public opinion.
    • External Scapegoating: Not yet occurred, impossible to judge.

Possible Crash Paths

Path One: Demand-side Collapse—Downstream revenue falls short of expectations, forcing upstream to issue bonds to maintain expenditures, ultimately triggering a bond run.

Downstream companies find that AI adoption does not bring sufficient profits and choose to stop or downgrade services, leading to a decline in revenue for upstream service providers, who can only rely on continuous bond issuance to maintain model training and hardware expenditures. Debt cannot be issued indefinitely. Once the market turns pessimistic about AI prospects, it could trigger a run on related bonds, leading to a crash.

  • Signs already observed: Companies like Uber have started limiting internal AI usage, shifting from "use freely" to "use judiciously"; corporate attitudes towards token consumption have shifted.
  • Additional judgment: The true trigger point is often not "slowly cooling sentiment" itself, but when this cooling coincides with a specific debt/lease maturity, requiring refinancing but unable to issue new debt.

Path Two: Single Node Failure, Cascading Through Highly Coupled Circular Structure (analogous to a Lehman-style trigger)

If any node in the circular funding system fails, it will quickly transmit to all nodes through the chain of "OpenAI drives Microsoft spending → Microsoft drives Nvidia orders → Nvidia drives data center construction."

  • Signs already observed (Oracle): Long-term debt increased 40% year-over-year to approximately $124.7 billion, free cash flow turned negative $23.7 billion; 5-year credit default swap spreads soared to 198 basis points, a historical high; of $523 billion in future performance obligations, at least $300 billion is concentrated in a single customer, OpenAI; another $248 billion in off-balance-sheet leasing commitments; sued by a pension fund in January 2026, alleging misleading bond issuance disclosures; the Bank for International Settlements' June 2026 annual report compared this round of AI capital expenditure to railroad mania, canal mania, and the dot-com bubble, specifically naming Oracle's Stargate project as a core case.
  • Early signs of loosening also observed: Microsoft has quietly abandoned its commitment to "priority fulfillment" of OpenAI's computing power demands, shifting to other cloud providers like Oracle to fill the gap.
  • Mitigating factors: Approximately 70% of Oracle's debt matures after 2030, so short-term repayment pressure is manageable, but this pushes and amplifies the risk to a future refinancing window.

Path Three: External Macro Shock (sudden change in interest rate environment), unrelated to AI's inherent quality (compared to Mexico in 1994, dot-com bubble in 2000)

If the Federal Reserve is forced to raise interest rates due to inflation or other reasons, it will directly increase the financing costs of the low-cost debt and private credit that this system heavily relies on (approximately half of global data center spending relies on private credit). The characteristic of this path is that the trigger factors are entirely exogenous, unrelated to AI technology itself, and most difficult for the industry itself to predict and control.

  • Current status: Although current US Treasury bonds do not support interest rate hikes, some country might unexpectedly default first, triggering a chain reaction (perhaps Japan/South Korea/Europe).

Path Four: "DeepSeek 2.0"—Technological progress itself becomes the trigger.

If a more thorough similar event occurs in the future (an open-source model's performance matches that of top closed-source models at only one-tenth the cost, and this time is not overshadowed by the optimistic narrative of "Jevons' paradox"), it could directly shake the foundation of the entire circular funding system based on "computing power demand will continue to grow explosively."

  • Already observed rehearsals and signs: DeepSeek shock in January 2025 (Nvidia plunged 17% in one day, approximately $600 billion evaporated, rebounded 8.8% the next day); May 2026 data showed that Chinese open-source models (DeepSeek V4 Pro, Zhipu GLM-5.2) could match top Western closed-source models at significantly lower costs, and over 60% of token consumption on the OpenRouter platform flowed to Chinese open-source models.
  • Two interpretations coexist: One believes this compresses model layer profits and is a risk signal; the other believes it confirms "Jevons' paradox"—that falling costs will only stimulate greater usage, which is beneficial for overall demand.

Common underlying judgment: Regardless of the specific trigger path, historical patterns suggest that the trigger point itself is usually not the root cause, but merely the last straw; the true root cause is the existing gap between "book promises (future revenue, circular revenue)" and "realizable assets (actual cash flow, real profits)." This gap has now been quantified (annual expenditure scale of $700 billion, consumer AI revenue of approximately $12 billion). The difference between the paths lies only in "how and when" the market collectively realizes this gap.
